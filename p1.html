Neighborly

Project OPUS: Platform Transformation

Neighborly: One Platform for 20+ Franchise Brands

UX strategy, information architecture, design system, and native app for the parent company of Molly Maid, Mosquito Joe, Mr. Rooter, Mr. Electric, and more.



The problem

Each brand Neighborly acquired came with its own website, booking system, and franchise operating model. Customers got a different experience every time, and nobody had ever mapped how a single customer journey crosses the end customer, the franchise owner’s point-of-service platform, and Neighborly’s central Call-a-Neighbor system.


What I did

I framed the work around three customer jobs (Repair, Maintain, Enhance), aligned 20+ brand stakeholders on one journey model, led the design system that let every brand keep its identity on shared components, and carried the same framework into the native app.

Chapter 01. -

Strategy

Untangling the Hairball: One Customer Journey Across 20+ Brands

My role

Led UX strategy, research framing, and cross-brand alignment

Partners

Brand presidents, PMOs, Engineering, Marketing, SEO, CRO

Timeline

Discovery and workshops took 2-3 Weeks on a rolling per-brand cadence

Result

Sign-off on one shared journey model; 60% fewer conflicting requirements in OPUS design reviews



The problem

Every brand defined “the customer journey” through its own tools. A single booking touches three organizational lenses at once (the customer, the franchise owner’s point-of-service platform, and Neighborly’s Call-a-Neighbor platform), and none had ever been mapped as one system.


Decision 1: Start from jobs, not screens


I framed the work around three jobs a customer is trying to get done: Repair, Maintain, Enhance. Each demands a different tone and level of support from the brand. This became our shared vocabulary. When a brand president asked why the booking flow didn’t work like theirs, the answer was usually “that’s a different job.”


I built personas on Neighborly’s real customer segmentation rather than invented archetypes. Two mental models became a lens for every flow: Diane (“show me options”) and Elizabeth (“decide for me and I’ll review”).

Decision 2: Map the system, not just the customer


The core deliverable was a six-stage journey map (Needs & Planning → Research & Compare → Books Service → Pre-Service → During Service → Post-Service). The fourth row, Behind the Scenes, tied every customer activity to the platform doing the work. I also defined the trigger for each stage change (for example, “franchise owner sends estimate” moves a customer from Research to Book), so engineering could use the map directly instead of treating it as a workshop artifact.

Decision 3: Group brands by job, not by brand


Rather than negotiating with 20+ brands one at a time, I ran three working sessions, one per job category (Repair, Maintain, Enhance), with all the brands in each category in the room together. Each session used the same structure: Stage → Goal → Activity → Touchpoint → Platform. Because input landed in one format, we could compare brands side by side and stop debating whose booking process was “right.”

  • Working sessions held: 3 sessions across 20+ brands (Repair, Maintain, Enhance Categories)
  • Brand presidents signed off on the shared model, with a rolling migration schedule of 15 waves, one every 3 weeks
  • Conflicting requirements entering OPUS design reviews fell by 60%

A catch that mattered


While mapping, I noticed that one booking (a cleaning) triggers parallel paths through the central platform and two franchise brands’ field operations, each with its own scheduling and lead-routing logic. Flagging it early meant the prototype and build accounted for multi-brand routing from the start, instead of surfacing in QA.


Results

Metric

Shared journey model signed off by brand presidents

All

Requirement-clarification rounds per brand before design started

~4 → ~1

13 of 20




















Chapter 02. -

Platform

Project OPUS: A Design System That Let 20+ Brands Keep Their Identity

My role

UX Lead on OPUS; led design execution, the design system, and the blended design team

Team

10 people across in-house design, research, accessibility, and an offshore agency

Platform

CrownPeak CMS, Tailwind CSS, Flowbite (heavily customized), Figma

Result

20+ brand sites on one token model and component library, with a signoff gate that made “done” mean the same thing everywhere



The problem

Chapter 1 defined what the experience needed to be. This chapter covers the system that let it get designed, reviewed, and shipped brand after brand without starting from zero each time.

Decision 1: Enforce structure, not appearance


Every brand runs the same token structure (Primary, Secondary, Accent 1, Accent 2), but the values are brand-specific. A brand president sees their brand; engineering builds against one model. I chose Tailwind and Flowbite as a starting point rather than an off-the-shelf answer, because the foundation had to flex across 20+ identities.

Decision 2: Solve navigation first


Navigation had to scale across every brand’s service structure without becoming a bloated mega-menu or an oversimplified one. I defined the principles on Mr. Electric first, and every later brand’s navigation had to pass the same test: mobile-first, an easy path to requesting service, simple and logical structure, clean use of white space, and consistency with room for brand personality.

Decision 3: Build once, reuse everywhere


Standard components (heroes, service grids, offer carousels, testimonial blocks, forms, location capture) were built once against the token system. New brands got most of their experience “for free,” which made the migration schedule realistic.

Decision 4: Turn gaps into permanent additions


Not every brand fit the library. We called these gaps deltas. Each one was designed, reviewed, and folded back into the shared system instead of living as a one-off exception.

Decision 5: Make “done” mean the same thing everywhere


Every sprint followed the same cadence: design, internal review across the blended team, then a formal signoff gate with product and brand stakeholders before anything went to engineering in CrownPeak. With 20+ stakeholders and a mixed internal and vendor team, informal review would have meant every brand renegotiating quality on its own terms.

Proof at scale


Mr. Electric (emergency electrical work) and ShelfGenie (considered home-storage consultations) don’t read like the same product: different color, tone, and service model. Both are assembled from the same components, tokens, and navigation principles.


Results

Metric

Brand sites on one platform

20+

Design-to-signoff time per brand, wave 1 → wave 6 *

~10 weeks → ~3 weeks

Components reused across brands

~80%

Design review rounds per brand before signoff

~4 → ~2

Accessibility issues found at pre-launch audit

−85% from wave 1 to wave 6



* Wave 1 was the expensive one, on purpose. The first migration took about 10 weeks because it was where we worked out the process by trial and error. Every wave after that fed what we learned back into the components, the deltas, and the signoff process, and by wave 6 the same work took about 3 weeks.

Looking ahead


I also scoped a longer-term scenario in which AI and smart-home data shift Neighborly from reactive booking to proactive service (a leak sensor triggering a repair booking, for example). I was explicit about what was in and out of scope for the near-term demo, so the exploration informed the roadmap without derailing current sprints.
